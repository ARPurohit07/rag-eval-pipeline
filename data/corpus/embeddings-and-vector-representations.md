# Embeddings and Vector Representations

An embedding is a mapping from a discrete or high-dimensional object — a word, a sentence, a document, an image — to a dense vector of real numbers in a lower-dimensional space, chosen so that geometric relationships in that space (distance, direction) reflect semantic relationships in the original data. The classic illustration is word2vec's observation that vector arithmetic on word embeddings can capture analogies, such as the vector for "king" minus "man" plus "woman" landing close to the vector for "queen."

Modern text embeddings are usually produced by encoder models trained with a contrastive objective: the model is trained so that embeddings of semantically similar texts (e.g., a question and its correct answer, or two paraphrases) are pulled close together in vector space, while embeddings of unrelated texts are pushed apart. Sentence-embedding models such as the Sentence-BERT family, E5, GTE, and BGE are trained this way on large collections of paired or weakly-paired text, often combining a general contrastive pretraining stage with task-specific fine-tuning (for example, fine-tuning specifically for retrieval).

The most common way to compare two embeddings is cosine similarity, which measures the cosine of the angle between two vectors and ignores their magnitude — this is preferred over raw dot product or Euclidean distance for text embeddings because it makes similarity invariant to how "long" a document's embedding happens to be, which can vary with text length or encoder quirks. Many embedding models are explicitly trained so that cosine similarity between their outputs is a meaningful similarity score, and some libraries normalize embeddings to unit length so that cosine similarity and dot product become equivalent.

Embedding dimensionality is a practical trade-off: higher-dimensional embeddings (768, 1024, or more) can encode more nuanced distinctions but cost more to store and search over, while smaller embeddings (384 or less) are cheaper and faster but may lose some discriminative power. Techniques like Matryoshka Representation Learning train a single embedding model so that truncating its output vector to a shorter prefix still yields a usable (if slightly less accurate) embedding, letting one model serve multiple storage/accuracy trade-offs.

A common failure mode in retrieval systems is embedding models struggling with out-of-domain vocabulary — a general-purpose embedding model trained mostly on web text may not distinguish well between two sentences that differ only in specialized technical or legal terminology, because it never learned that those terms carry different meaning. This is one reason domain-specific fine-tuning, or combining semantic embeddings with lexical methods like BM25, often improves retrieval quality over semantic search alone.
