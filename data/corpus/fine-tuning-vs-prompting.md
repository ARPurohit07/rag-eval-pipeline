# Fine-Tuning, Prompt Engineering, and RAG: Choosing an Approach

There are three broad ways to adapt a pretrained language model to a specific task or knowledge domain, and they are not mutually exclusive: prompt engineering, retrieval-augmented generation, and fine-tuning. Choosing between them is a question of what kind of adaptation is actually needed — a new skill or behavior, versus new knowledge, versus a narrow output format — and each has a different cost profile.

Prompt engineering adapts model behavior purely through the text of the input at inference time: instructions, examples (few-shot prompting), and formatting requirements, with no change to the model's weights. It is the cheapest and fastest option to iterate on, requires no training infrastructure, and works within a single API call, but it is bounded by the context window and by what the base model is already capable of — a prompt cannot teach a model facts it was never exposed to, and very long, complex instructions can be inconsistently followed by weaker models.

Retrieval-augmented generation adapts the model's effective knowledge without touching its weights, by supplying relevant external information in-context at query time, as covered in the RAG document. It is the natural choice when the task is fundamentally about answering questions over a specific, possibly large or frequently changing, body of knowledge — because that knowledge can be updated by re-indexing documents, with no retraining required, and answers can be grounded in retrievable, citable sources.

Fine-tuning updates the model's weights on a task-specific dataset, so the adaptation is baked into the model itself rather than supplied at inference time. It is the right tool when the goal is to change how the model behaves — its tone, its output format, its ability to follow a domain-specific instruction schema, or a narrow skill like structured extraction — rather than what it knows. Full fine-tuning updates all of a model's parameters and is expensive in compute and data; parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods such as LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) instead freeze the original weights and train small, low-rank update matrices injected into specific layers, dramatically reducing the number of trainable parameters and the memory needed, at some cost in achievable adaptation capacity compared to full fine-tuning.

A common misconception is treating fine-tuning as a way to "teach the model new facts" as a substitute for retrieval: fine-tuning on a document corpus does tend to shift a model's outputs toward that corpus's style and can improve recall of frequently repeated facts, but it does not reliably give the model the ability to answer arbitrary questions about the corpus's long tail of specific details the way retrieval does, and it offers no way to cite a source or update a single fact without retraining. In practice, RAG and fine-tuning are complementary: a model might be fine-tuned to reliably follow a particular answer format or to better use retrieved context (sometimes called retrieval-aware fine-tuning), while still relying on RAG for up-to-date, citable factual grounding.
