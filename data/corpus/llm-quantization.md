# LLM Quantization

Quantization reduces the numerical precision used to store and compute a neural network's weights (and sometimes activations), for example converting 16-bit floating point weights to 8-bit or 4-bit integers, in order to shrink the model's memory footprint and speed up inference, usually at some cost in output quality. For large language models, quantization is often the difference between a model requiring multiple high-end GPUs and the same model running on a single consumer GPU or even a laptop CPU.

Post-training quantization (PTQ) converts an already-trained model's weights to lower precision without further training, typically using a small calibration dataset to determine good scaling factors per weight tensor (or per smaller group of weights, in "group-wise" quantization) that minimize the numerical error introduced by rounding to fewer bits. Methods such as GPTQ and AWQ are popular PTQ techniques specifically designed for Transformer LLMs: GPTQ quantizes weights layer by layer, adjusting remaining unquantized weights to compensate for the error introduced in already-quantized weights, while AWQ observes that a small fraction of weights are disproportionately important (based on the magnitude of the activations they interact with) and preserves those at higher precision while aggressively quantizing the rest.

Quantization-aware training (QAT) instead simulates the effect of quantization during training or fine-tuning itself, so the model's weights are optimized to be robust to the precision reduction that will be applied later. QAT typically achieves better quality at a given bit-width than PTQ, because the model gets to adapt to quantization noise during training rather than having it applied as an afterthought, but it requires a full (or fine-tuning-scale) training run rather than a one-time post-hoc conversion, making it more expensive to apply.

GGUF is a file format (used by the llama.cpp project and, by extension, Ollama) that packages a quantized model's weights, tokenizer, and metadata into a single file, with a naming convention (like Q4_K_M or Q8_0) indicating the quantization scheme and bit-width used. Lower-numbered quantizations (like Q4) are smaller and faster but lose more quality; higher ones (like Q8) are closer to the original full-precision model but larger. Running a "4-bit" quantized 7-billion-parameter model typically requires roughly 4-5 GB of memory rather than the ~14 GB a 16-bit version would need, which is why quantization is what makes running capable LLMs locally on consumer hardware practical at all.

Quantization is not free: aggressive quantization (very low bit-widths, or quantizing without a good calibration process) can measurably degrade a model's reasoning ability, its faithfulness to long context, and its calibration (how well its confidence matches its actual accuracy), even when it still produces fluent-looking text — which is why quantized models used in production, especially as an LLM-as-judge in an evaluation pipeline, should be spot-checked against the full-precision model's outputs rather than assumed to be equivalent.
