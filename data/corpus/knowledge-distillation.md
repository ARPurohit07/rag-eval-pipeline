# Knowledge Distillation

Knowledge distillation is a technique for training a smaller "student" model to reproduce the behavior of a larger, more capable "teacher" model, with the goal of retaining as much of the teacher's quality as possible in a model that is cheaper and faster to run. It is a distinct technique from quantization: quantization reduces the numerical precision of an existing model's weights, while distillation trains a genuinely smaller model (fewer parameters, and often a different architecture entirely) from scratch or from a smaller pretrained base, using the teacher's outputs as a training signal. The two are complementary and are often applied together — a distilled model can subsequently be quantized as well.

The classic formulation of distillation, introduced by Hinton et al., trains the student not just to match the teacher's single top prediction (a "hard label") but to match the teacher's full output probability distribution over all possible outputs (a "soft label"). The intuition is that a teacher's probability distribution carries more information than its single most likely answer — if a teacher assigns 70% probability to "cat" and 25% to "dog" and 5% to everything else for a given image, that relative ranking and those relative magnitudes convey which mistakes are "reasonable" (confusing cat and dog) versus "unreasonable" (confusing cat and truck), information a single hard label throws away. A temperature parameter is used to soften the teacher's distribution (making the non-top probabilities larger and more informative) before computing the training loss against the student.

For large language models specifically, distillation commonly takes the form of using a large, expensive model to generate a large dataset of high-quality outputs (answers, chain-of-thought reasoning traces, or preference judgments) for a wide variety of prompts, and then fine-tuning a smaller model on that generated dataset with ordinary supervised fine-tuning — sometimes called "sequence-level" or "black-box" distillation, since it only requires access to the teacher's text outputs rather than its internal probability distributions or weights, which is important when the teacher is only available behind an API. This is the approach used to produce many small, capable open-weight models that closely imitate the style and, to a significant extent, the capability of a much larger closed model on similar prompts.

Distillation has practical limits: a small student model has fundamentally less capacity than a large teacher, so it cannot perfectly replicate the teacher's behavior on the hardest tasks in the teacher's distribution, and a student trained heavily on a teacher's outputs can inherit the teacher's specific errors, stylistic quirks, and blind spots just as reliably as it inherits its strengths. Distilled models also tend to generalize somewhat less well outside the specific distribution of prompts they were distilled on, compared to a model of similar size trained directly on diverse human-generated data, which is why distillation datasets are usually constructed to be broad and diverse rather than narrowly focused on a single task.
